# Sources: 006 How Charles Ponzi Turned a 5-Cent Coupon Into $15 Million (Charles Ponzi, Boston 1919-1920)

## Sources read
1. "In Ponzi We Trust", Mary Darby, Smithsonian Magazine (editor's note 14 Apr 2021), https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/in-ponzi-we-trust-64016168/. Five foot two; arrived Boston 15 Nov 1903 on the SS Vancouver; a cardsharp took most of his money on the crossing; quote "I landed in this country with $2.50 in cash and $1 million in hopes"; waiter/busboy in New York, sign painter in Florida, odd jobs on the East Coast; Boston 1917 as a clerk for broker J. R. Poole; married Rose Gnecco Feb 1918, took over her father's grocery and made a mess of it; trade-journal idea, Hanover Trust refused a $2,000 loan; August 1919, a Spanish correspondent's letter with an international reply coupon bought for 30 centavos, swappable in the US for a 5-cent stamp, peseta had fallen; Securities Exchange Company; first 50% in 90 days, later 50% in 45 days; agents 10%, subagents 5%; many reinvested; "some 40,000 people"; "an estimated $15 million in eight months"; 12-room Lexington mansion, servants, chauffeur-driven Locomobile, gold-handled Malacca canes, diamonds for Rose; quote "The more I bought, the more I wanted to buy... It was a mania"; took over Hanover Trust; Joseph Daniels' $1 million suit (July 1920); Boston Post front page 24 July 1920 "DOUBLES THE MONEY WITHIN THREE MONTHS"; Monday 26 July 1920 line from City Hall Annex to School Street; publicist William McMasters advised the audit and stopping new deposits at noon 26 July; Post reported more than $1 million paid out in one day; coffee and sandwiches, fainting women moved to the front; quotes "My secret is how to cash the coupons..." and "Let the United States find it out, if it can"; 2 Aug McMasters in the Post: "hopelessly insolvent", over $2 million in debt, at least $4.5 million with interest; 11 Aug Post story on his Canadian forgery term with Montreal mugshots; Atlanta federal term for smuggling five Italians from Canada; auditor Edwin Pride: $3 million deficit, later $7 million; jailer: "The man's nerve is iron"; half a dozen banks crashed; note holders got under 30 cents on the dollar; Dunn: final audit found $61 of coupons; federal mail-fraud conviction, served 3.5 years; 1925 state conviction; Florida swampland as "Charpon"; jumped bail, seaman on an Italian freighter, captured in New Orleans; released 1934 "balding and 40 pounds heavier", never a citizen, deported 7 Oct; Rose divorced him later; Italy-Brazil airline job (Dunn's account); stroke 1948, failing eyesight; died 18 Jan 1949, charity hospital in Rio de Janeiro, left $75 for his burial; Galbraith on "rediscovering some earlier form of fraud". High reliability.
2. "When Ponzi's Bubble Burst", Prologue Magazine (Summer 2010, Vol. 42 No. 2), US National Archives, https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2010/summer/ponzi-inmate-case-file. Late 1919 pitch: buy reply coupons in weak-currency countries, redeem in the US; 50% in 90 days; roughly $15 million in eight months; early investors paid with later investors' money; Post Office investigating; the Post revealed his two prison terms 1908-1912 (Canada, check forgery; US, bringing five Italians in from Canada); his Atlanta inmate case file holds the Post's 1920 telegram and a 1910 mug shot. High reliability (primary-record based).
3. "Ponzi Scheme", Behind the Badge exhibition, Smithsonian National Postal Museum / US Postal Inspection Service, https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/behind-the-badge-case-histories-scams-and-schemes/ponzi-scheme. Reply coupons created by the Universal Postal Union in 1906, swappable for postage in another country; post-war devaluations; 50% in 45 days; postal inspectors found world coupon sales nowhere near enough to support his profits; meeting with authorities Monday 26 July 1920, audit, no new money; 9 Aug 1920 Bank Commissioner Joseph Allen told Hanover Trust to stop honouring his cheques; pleaded guilty to using the mails to defraud (Nov 1920), five years; two state acquittals, then a Feb 1925 conviction (7-9 years); Florida securities conviction; deported to Italy 7 Oct 1934. High reliability.
4. "What Journalism Owes to Charles Ponzi", Roy J. Harris Jr. (author of "Pulitzer's Gold"), Poynter, 17 Jan 2009, https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2009/what-journalism-owes-to-charles-ponzi/. 50% in 90 days, later halved; $1 buys 5 coupons in Boston or 66 in Rome after exchange into lira; quote about the "little dollar" that comes home "in six weeks, married and with a couple of kids"; Post editor/publisher Richard Grozier; Clarence Barron (Boston News Bureau, Dow Jones, The Wall Street Journal) quote that no experienced investor "would look twice" at 50% in three months; Montreal tip about a "Charles Ponsi" jailed for forgery, job at Banco Zarossi, "I might have"; reporter Herbert Baldwin took a photo to Montreal, witnesses said it matched with a moustache, "Bianchi, the snake!"; 11 Aug 1920 story; Pulitzer Prize for Public Service 1921; Ponzi: "the old game of robbing Peter to pay Paul". High reliability.
5. "A dreamer as much as a schemer", Jessica Ullian, B.U. Bridge, 25 Mar 2005, https://www.bu.edu/bridge/archive/2005/03-25/zuckoff.html (on Mitchell Zuckoff's "Ponzi's Scheme", Random House 2005). Born Lugo, Italy, 1882; to the US 1903; jailed for cheque forgery and for smuggling aliens; Boston 1916, married Rose; scheme launched January 1920; by July 1920 about 20,000 people had invested more than $9 million; never redeemed a coupon; 50% in 45 days; uncovered about eight months in, chiefly through Richard Grozier of the Post. High reliability.
6. "The Boston Post", Pulitzer Prizes, https://www.pulitzer.org/winners/boston-post. 1921 prize "For its exposure of the operations of Charles Ponzi by a series of articles which finally led to his arrest." Primary.

## How the script handles disputes
- Promised return: 50% in 90 days (Prologue, Poynter, Barron) vs 50% in 45 days (Postal Museum, BU). Smithsonian and Poynter explain both: it began at 90 days and was cut to 45. The script tells it that way.
- When it began: "late 1919" (Prologue), "by December the money had begun to roll in" (Smithsonian), "launched in January 1920" (BU). The script says "around the end of nineteen nineteen".
- Money and investors: about $15 million in eight months (Smithsonian, Prologue) vs more than $9 million from about 20,000 people by July (BU). Smithsonian says some 40,000 people. The script says "by most estimates, some fifteen million dollars" and "by one count, some forty thousand people".
- Prison history: Prologue gives Canada 1908-1910 (forgery) and US 1910-1912 (five Italians), Poynter mentions Montreal and Georgia; the script says Montreal (forged cheque) and Atlanta.
- "$61 of coupons" comes from one author's (Dunn's) account in the Smithsonian piece; the script says "by one account".
- Later life (airline job in Brazil) varies between accounts; the script says "accounts differ... by one".
- Deficit: $3 million, later revised to $7 million (Smithsonian); the script gives both.
- He "did not invent the trick": paying old investors with new money predates him (Galbraith, quoted in Smithsonian, on fraudsters "rediscovering some earlier form of fraud").
