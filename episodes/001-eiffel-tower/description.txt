In 1925, a con man convinced a Paris scrap-metal dealer that the French government was secretly selling the Eiffel Tower for scrap. He walked away with a fortune. Then he came back and tried to sell it again.

This is the true story of Victor Lustig, the con man who sold the Eiffel Tower. He called himself a count. He wasn't one. He was a professional liar with dozens of aliases and one rare talent: working out exactly what a person wanted to believe.

We follow Lustig from the transatlantic ocean liners where he sold a "money box" that supposedly printed $100 bills, to the forged government letter that lured Paris's scrap dealers to the Hôtel de Crillon, to the bribe that turned a suspicious buyer into a believer. Then America: a con on Al Capone himself, a counterfeiting operation pushing more than $100,000 in fake bills a month, a bedsheet escape from a Manhattan jail, and finally Alcatraz.

It's one of the most famous cons in history, and the psychology behind it still works today. Watch closely for the moment Lustig cures his buyer's doubt by confessing to a smaller crime.
