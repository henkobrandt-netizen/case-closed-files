## Fact sheet (every fact with source number[s])

### MacGregor background
- Born 24 Dec 1786 at Glengyle, Stirlingshire, per Sinclair [1][9]. The ODNB and MacGregor's own 1826 statement say Edinburgh [1]. His father Daniel was an East India Company sea captain who died in 1794 [1][6].
- Joined the British Army in April 1803, aged 16. His family bought him an ensign's commission in the 57th Foot for about £450 [1]. Became lieutenant in Feb 1804 [1] and bought a captaincy for about £900 in 1805 [1][6].
- Married Maria Bowater, a Royal Navy admiral's daughter, in June 1805 [1][6]. She died in Dec 1811 [1][3].
- Peninsular War: the 57th landed at Lisbon on 15 July 1809. He served as a major with the 8th Line Battalion of the Portuguese Army (Oct 1809–Apr 1810) [1][6]. Retired 24 May 1810 [1].
- Later falsely implied he had fought at Albuera (16 May 1811), a year after leaving the regiment [1]. Claimed descent from Rob Roy [7].
- Venezuela: arrived at La Guaira in April 1812. Miranda made him a brigadier-general by June 1812 [1][6]. On 10 June 1812 he married Josefa Antonia Andrea Aristeguieta y Lovera, a cousin of Bolívar [1][3][4][6].
- 1816 retreat from La Cabrera: 34 days, reaching Barcelona on 20 Aug 1816. Bolívar called it "superior to the conquest of an empire" [1][6].
- Amelia Island, Florida: landed 29 June 1817, and the Spanish fort (51 men) surrendered without a shot. Proclaimed a "Republic of the Floridas" and left in Sept 1817 [1][3][4]. Reportedly raised $160,000 selling Florida land "scrip" first [7].
- Porto Bello: Colonel Rafter took the town on 10 Apr 1819 and the Spanish retook it on 30 Apr. MacGregor fled to sea, abandoning about 200 men [1]. Rio de la Hacha (Oct 1819): his force had fallen from about 900 to about 250. He stayed on his ship and left the troops behind [1]. Both 1819 operations ended with him "abandoning his British volunteers" [3][4].

### The Mosquito Coast land grant
- Date: 29 April 1820, at Cape Gracias a Dios [1][2][3].
- Grantor: King George Frederic Augustus of the Mosquito (Miskitu) people [1][3][7]. Clavel calls him "George Frederic" [2].
- Size: 8,000,000 acres, about 12,500 sq mi or 32,375 km², "larger than Wales" [1][3][4][5][7][9]. Clavel gives "over 33,000 km², about the size of current Moldova" [2].
- Payment: rum and jewellery [1][3].
- "Poyais" comes from the Paya (Poyer) people [1].
- Clavel: the grant did **not** cede sovereignty, and was a strategic act by the king rather than a drunken giveaway [2].
- The king's revocation of the grant was printed in *The Times* on 1 Sept 1823 [2].

### The invented country
- Title: "Cazique of Poyais", used in London from mid-1821 [1][3]. Also styled "His Highness Gregor" [4] and "Gregor the First" on bond certificates [9]. Sinclair: the title and Poyais as a state were his own invention [1][3].
- Flag: a green cross on a white field, the same flag used at Amelia [1]. Sinclair calls it "a green cross of St George on a white background" [10]. Coat of arms supported by Poyers and unicorns [1]. Also regalia, army uniforms and a tricameral government [9].
- Guidebook: *Sketch of the Mosquito Shore, Including the Territory of Poyais*, 355 pp., Edinburgh and London, mid-1822, by "Captain Thomas Strangeways", aide-de-camp to the Cazique and captain in the "1st Native Poyer Regiment" [1][10]. Probably written by MacGregor or his associates [1][2].
- Guidebook claims: climate "remarkably healthy" [1][3]; three maize harvests a year [1][10]; one day's hunting feeds a family for a week [1]; rivers with "globules of pure gold" [1][7]; a gold mine called "Albrapoyer" [7]; natives "modest, docile and friendly", possibly of Aztec descent [10]; no income tax [10]; an average heat of about 80 degrees with sea breezes [10]; Indian labour at about £13–16 a year [10].
- Capital St Joseph: up to 20,000 inhabitants, with a theatre, opera house, domed cathedral, Bank of Poyais, parliament and royal palace, almost all fictional [1]. Also "public buildings and an opera house" and a deep-water port [4].
- Sold offices: commissions in the Poyais army, bank posts, and a "royal shoemaker" [4][7]. Mauger, a city financier, was named manager of the Bank of Poyais [1][7].
- Banknotes: 5,000 Bank of Poyais dollar notes from the Bank of Scotland's official printer were handed to Mauger on 10 Sept 1822 (Sinclair, via [1]); "printed in Scotland" [4][7]. Emigrants swapped sterling and gold for them [6][7].
- Land offices operated in London, Edinburgh and Glasgow [3][6][10]. The Glasgow *Sentinel* ran adverts on "The Face of the Country", listing mahogany, cedar, zebra wood and pitch pine [10].

### The London loan (1822) and money raised
- Offered 23 Oct 1822, underwritten by Sir John Perring, Shaw, Barber & Co. [1][2].
- Amount: £200,000, as 2,000 bonds of £100 [1][2][8]. Wiki also lists £200 and £500 bonds [1].
- Issue price: 80 [1][2][8]. Payable £15 on delivery, then £35 on 17 Jan 1823 and £30 on 14 Feb 1823 [2] (Wiki gives a 15% deposit with the balance due on 17 Jan and 14 Feb [1]).
- Interest: 6% a year, secured on "all the revenues of the Government of Poyais" [1][2][9]. Taylor says 3% [8].
- Traded on the LSE from 29 Oct 1822 and reached 86 by the end of October [2]. Wigglesworth quotes a price of 86.5 and a yield of about 6.9% [9].
- Contractor John Lowe took about 8% commission and Perring's firm probably 5% [2]. Lowe went to Paris with about a third of the roughly £30,000 first instalment [2]. Later instalments were postponed and cut to £10, then £5 [2].
- MacGregor had grossed about £50,000 by 1823 [1].
- A second £200,000 loan was floated in Oct 1823 (Perring again) but "failed to sell many bonds" [1][5].
- The bonds fell to £5–£20 after the king's revocation appeared in *The Times* (Sept 1823) [2], and to under 10% of face value by late 1823 [1]. On 9 Nov 1824 the Foreign Stock Market committee ruled Poyais securities "non-cognizable" [2].
- Context: Gran Colombia raised a £2m loan in March 1822 [1]. British Consols paid about 3% [3] or under 4% [9]. More than two dozen foreign governments sold bonds in London in 1818–1832, often yielding over 7% [9]. The Panic of 1825 was "arguably the first true global financial crisis" [9]. Straney calls MacGregor the "founding father of [securities fraud]" [1].

### Land sales
- Initially 2s 3d an acre, raised to 2s 6d in July 1822 and then gradually to 4s [1].
- 100 acres for just over £11 [4][7], which matches 2s 3d an acre.
- Taylor: 3s 3d an acre, later 4s, "about a day's wages" [8].
- 1828: "Land in Poyais Proper" at 5s an acre [1][3][8]. Further certificates in 1834 and 1837 [3][8].
- About 500 people had bought land by early 1823, per MacGregor's claim [1]. Certificates eventually covered at least half of the 8m acres [1].

### Settler ships
- *Honduras Packet*: left London 10 Sept 1822 with about 70 emigrants under Hector Hall, and reached the Black River in Nov 1822 [1][3][7][8].
- *Kennersley Castle* (Capt. Henry Crouch): left Leith 22 Jan 1823 with almost 200 emigrants [1][3][8][10]. Mental Floss says 14 Jan [7]. Landed late March 1823 [1].
- *Skene* (105 Scots): found the Black River site abandoned and went on to Belize [1]. *Albion*: arrived at Belize in Nov 1823 with stores, which were auctioned [1].
- Goods worth about £16,000 were shipped [2].
- The Royal Navy intercepted five more emigrant ships [1][9].

### What they found at the Black River
- No St Joseph. A search party found only old foundations and rubble [1]. They found malarial swamp and jungle [4][5][7] and built makeshift shelters on the shore [5].
- The *Honduras Packet* sailed away with supplies still aboard [1]. A canoe party tried to reach Belize (about 500 nautical miles) and foundered, and one man drowned [1].
- Malaria and yellow fever came with the rainy season [1][9].
- A cobbler promised the post of "Shoemaker to the Princess" shot himself [7]. Wigglesworth also mentions suicides [9].
- Survivor James Hastie: "Sickness and despondency was so general, that few were able or willing to make any exertion" [4].

### Rescue and deaths
- Rescue: the *Mexican Eagle* carried Marshal Bennett, Chief Magistrate of Belize, and found the settlers in early May 1823 [1][7] (May 1823 also in [4]). Clavel: a British Honduras schooner in April 1823 [2].
- Edward Codd, Superintendent of British Honduras, opened an inquiry [1][2]. About 40 settlers were too weak to leave [1]. Some stayed on as mahogany labourers [2].
- The *Ocean* left Belize on 1 Aug 1823, and survivors reached London on 12 Oct 1823 [1][8].
- Death toll (see disagreements): of about 250, at least 180 died and fewer than 50 returned [1][9].

### Press and reaction
- *Quarterly Review*, "The Poyais Bubble" [1][2]: Clavel dates its attack on the guide to Oct 1822 and links it to John Murray's Colombian-bond interests [2]. Wiki gives Feb 1823 (Vol. XXVIII) [1].
- *The Times*: June 1823 reported the settlers "died miserably" [2]. 1 Sept 1823: printed the king's revocation [2]. Other coverage in the *Morning Chronicle*, *Morning Post*, *Public Ledger* and *Observer* (21 Sept 1823) [2].
- On 22 Oct 1823 six survivors signed an affidavit blaming Hall and the agents, not MacGregor [1]. Richardson issued libel writs against newspapers on MacGregor's behalf [1]. A handbill read "Another Poyais Humbug" [1].
- MacGregor left for France shortly before the survivors arrived, saying he was taking Josefa to Italy for her health [3].

### France (1825–26)
- Compagnie de la Nouvelle Neustrie (managing director Lehuby) sought 500,000 acres [1][2]. Aug 1825: a republican constitution [1][4]. A £300,000 loan at 2.5% was announced on 18 Aug 1825 through Thomas Jenkins & Co., with no evidence it was issued [1].
- Hippisley and Irving were arrested in Paris on 4 Sept 1825 [1]. MacGregor was arrested on 7 Dec 1825 after about three months in hiding and held at La Force [1][8].
- Trial of 6 April 1826: charges withdrawn [1]. Trial of 10 July 1826: Lehuby got 13 months and MacGregor was acquitted on all counts [1][8]. "Released eight months later" [4]; "two months on remand" [8].

### Later London attempts
- Held about a week at Tothill Fields Bridewell, then released without charge [1].
- 1827: £800,000 loan at 3% via Thomas Jenkins & Co., mostly unsold [1][4][8]. 1830: rival claimant "King Robert Charles Frederic" issued certificates for the same land [1]. 1831: "Poyaisian New Three per cent Consolidated Stock" [8].
- Living in Edinburgh by 1834. 1836: constitution for a smaller Poyais republic. Last scheme 1837 [1][3].

### Venezuela and death
- Josefa died 4 May 1838 at Burghmuirhead, Edinburgh [1][3]. He applied to Venezuela in Oct 1838 and Páez approved it in March 1839 [1]. He was confirmed as citizen and divisional general with a pension of one-third salary [1][3][4].
- Died 4 Dec 1845 in Caracas, aged 58, and was buried with full military honours in Caracas Cathedral. President Soublette attended [1][3][6][8]. Obituaries praised the 1816 retreat and left out Poyais [1].
- Never convicted of any crime [4].

### Vivid details for animation
- **The banknote**: a black-and-white engraved Bank of Poyais $1 note by Lizars, "St. Joseph", with arms at top centre, the bank title at left, and "by order of his highness Gregor, Cazique of Poyais" at lower left. Only about five $1 notes are thought to survive. One proof sold for £1,500 [11]. The 5,000 notes reached Mauger on 10 Sept 1822, the same day the *Honduras Packet* sailed [1].
- **The bond**: elaborately decorated certificates promising that "Gregor the First" would repay from the state's revenues [9]. The flag was a green cross on white [1][10], and the arms had Poyer warriors and unicorns [1].
- **The guidebook**: rivers with "globules of pure gold" [1][7]; moonlight so bright "the smallest print is legible" [10]; the only picture the emigrants had was an engraving of Black River port, a "peaceful lagoon" under a Sugarloaf mountain [10].
- **The send-off**: Leith, Wed 22 Jan 1823. Hundreds of relatives on the dock, the Cazique comes aboard to announce free passage for women and children, and Crouch fires a six-gun broadside and runs up the Poyais flag. St Kilda fades astern on 27 Jan [10].
- **The arrival**: a search for St Joseph that finds only old foundations and rubble [1].

## Where sources disagree
- **Birth**: 24 Dec 1786 at Glengyle [1][9] vs Edinburgh (ODNB, via [1]) vs "Stirling, 24 Dec 1785" [12].
- **Grant size**: 8m acres [1][4][5][7] vs over 33,000 km² "about Moldova" [2]. These are close, but the round "8 million acres" is the standard figure.
- **Loan interest**: 6% [1][2][9] vs 3% [8]. Use 6%.
- **Loan price**: issued at 80 [1][2][8]. The 86 and 86.5 figures [2][9] are early market prices or another quote.
- **Loan date**: Oct 1822 [1][2][8][9]. History.com says 1821 [4], which is wrong.
- **Number of £200k loans**: one, plus a failed second in Oct 1823 [1]. Britannica says "two £200,000 loans" as if both succeeded [5]. STV says £300,000 via Thomas Jenkins [12], which conflates the 1825/27 issues.
- **Money actually raised**: about £50,000 gross by 1823 [1]; about £30,000 first instalment with a third taken by the broker [2]; "about £200,000 in one year" [6]; "several hundred thousand pounds" [4]; £1.3m [12]. Treat anything above about £50k cash as unproven.
- **Land price**: 2s 3d rising to 2s 6d then 4s [1]; 3s 3d rising to 4s [8]. £11 for 100 acres [4][7] fits 2s 3d.
- **Kennersley Castle departure**: 22 Jan 1823 [1][3][8][10] vs 14 Jan [7]. Sinclair's excerpt (blizzard delays, Wednesday 22 Jan) supports the 22nd.
- **Settler totals**: about 250 on the first two ships [1][9]; 240 who reached Poyais [3][8]; about 270 [7]; "over 200 on four ships" [2].
- **Death toll**: at least 180 dead and fewer than 50 home [1][9]; 60 survivors out of 240 [3][8]; "about two-thirds" [4][12]; "almost three-quarters", over 150 [5]; "over 150" [4, intro]; "nearly 200" [6]. A safe line: "of roughly 250, perhaps 180 died; fewer than 50 ever saw Britain again."
- **Rescue date and ship**: *Mexican Eagle*, early May 1823 [1][7] vs a schooner in April 1823 [2].
- **Quarterly Review "Poyais Bubble"**: Oct 1822 [2] vs Feb 1823 [1].
- **Josefa**: Bolívar's cousin [1][3][4][6] vs niece [12].
- **French custody**: about two months on remand [8] vs released "eight months later" [4]. Wiki's dates (arrest 7 Dec 1825, final acquittal 10 July 1826) span about seven months, with release possibly after the April hearing [1].
- **French money**: "almost £300,000 from French investors" [6][12] vs a £300k loan announced but no evidence any bonds were issued [1].
- **Is it a pure fraud?** Most sources say yes [1][4][5][9]. Clavel argues it was a real grant and a serious, flawed colonial-financial project, later "labeled" a fraud by British rivals, with a broker who stole funds [2].
- **"Strangeways"**: usually treated as fictional [1][2], but the 1825 Army List records a real Captain Thomas Strangeways [1].

## Phrases to hedge
- "Traded the land for rum and jewels" / "a drunken king": the rum and jewellery payment is reported [1][3], but Clavel disputes the drunkenness framing [2]. Say "reportedly in exchange for rum and jewellery."
- "Prince of Poyais": he styled himself Cazique, and "Prince" is the popular gloss [4][5]. "King" is wrong.
- "A country that never existed": the land and the grant were real. St Joseph, the opera house, the bank and the cathedral did not exist [1][2].
- Banknotes "printed by the Bank of Scotland": they came from the Bank of Scotland's *official printer* [1][7], not the Bank itself. Lizars is the engraver named on a surviving proof [11]. Do not assert these are the same firm without checking.
- "Fled with the money": he left for France in late 1823 [1][3], but how much cash he held is unclear (£50k gross [1], with the broker taking a share [2]).
- "Caused the Panic of 1825" / "first stock market crash": unsupported [12]. Say Poyais rode the Latin American bond boom that collapsed in 1825 [1][9].
- French officials "noticed a surge in passport applications" [7]: plausible story, single popular source. Hedge it.
- The cobbler "Shoemaker to the Princess" suicide [7]: single popular source, likely Sinclair-derived. Hedge with "one account says."
- Descent from Rob Roy [7]: MacGregor's claim, not a fact.
- "Greatest con of all time" is *The Economist*'s phrase (2012, via [1]), and "most audacious fraud in history" is Sinclair's subtitle [10]. Attribute both.
- The Hastie quote [4] comes from his published narrative. Cite as "settler James Hastie later wrote."
- Exact survivor and death numbers: always say "around" or "at least."
