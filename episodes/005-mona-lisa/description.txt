On a quiet Monday in August 1911, a man walked out of the Louvre with the Mona Lisa under his arm. A plumber held the door for him. Nobody noticed she was gone for more than a day.

This is the true story of the Mona Lisa theft of 1911, the heist that turned a quietly admired portrait into the most famous painting in the world. Before the theft, a Paris travel guide gave her just two sentences. The thief was Vincenzo Peruggia, a young Italian painter and decorator who had worked at the Louvre on the glass cases that were meant to protect its best paintings. He knew the rooms, the guards, the empty Monday, and exactly how that glass box came apart.

When the painting vanished, the Louvre closed for a week, travellers and ships were searched at the borders, and newspapers around the world ran the story. Then something strange happened: thousands of people queued to stare at an empty patch of wall and four iron hooks. The poet Guillaume Apollinaire was arrested and Pablo Picasso was questioned. Neither had anything to do with it. Meanwhile, the Mona Lisa sat in a trunk with a false bottom in a small rented room in Paris, for twenty-eight months.

How he got her out, how the police missed him, the letter signed "Leonardo", the meeting in a Florence hotel room, and why so many Italians called him a hero, step by step.
