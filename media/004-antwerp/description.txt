In February 2003, thieves emptied the most secure vault in the Antwerp diamond district without setting off a single alarm. The case came apart because of a bag of rubbish, and half of a salami sandwich.

This is the true story of the Antwerp diamond heist, often called the heist of the century. Leonardo Notarbartolo, an Italian gem dealer from Turin, rented an office in the Antwerp Diamond Centre in November 2000. Like every tenant, he got his own box in the vault, two floors underground, behind ten layers of security: a combination dial with 100 million combinations, a foot-long key, seismic and magnetic sensors, heat and light sensors, Doppler radar and cameras.

Over Valentine's Day weekend 2003, a crew later known as the School of Turin beat every one of those layers. Police counted 123 safe deposit boxes opened. Police and insurers put the loss at about $100 million; Notarbartolo says it was closer to $20 million, and that he was set up. Then, on the drive home, one nervous man threw a bag of rubbish into a forest owned by a retired grocer who hated litter.

How they got in, what the rubbish gave away, and why the diamonds have never been found, step by step.
