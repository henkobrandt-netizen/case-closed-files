In 1945, a Dutch painter was arrested for selling a priceless Vermeer to Hermann Göring. The penalty could be death. His only defence was a sentence nobody believed: "I painted it myself."

This is the true story of Han van Meegeren, the art forger who fooled the Nazis, the world's top experts and an entire country. Rejected by the critics, he spent six years inventing a way to make new paint look three hundred years old, using an oven and an early plastic called Bakelite. Then he painted the "lost" Vermeer the experts were hoping to find.

The Supper at Emmaus was hailed as Vermeer's masterpiece and bought by a Rotterdam museum. More fakes followed, a fortune followed, and in the middle of the Nazi occupation, one of his forgeries ended up in Göring's private collection, traded for 137 looted paintings.

To escape a treason charge, he had to prove he was a forger: by painting a new "Vermeer" under guard. He became a national hero. But historians who went back to the archives found that his last and greatest forgery was the hero story itself.
